The market guides the outcome

The market determines the achievable rent—not the property management company. We recommend a starting price, present and market the home, and evaluate the response, but renters decide whether the offering represents value compared with their other choices. We can begin at $4,350 as the upper-end test and should gain an early indication from qualified inquiries, completed showings and applications. We review that response after five days and reassess by days 10–14; no inquiry count or timeline guarantees a lease. The priority is effective rental income—what the property actually earns after vacancy and concessions. A higher asking rent is worthwhile only when the added income exceeds the cost of waiting.

Why test $4,350—and let the market respond

The reported quick lease at $4,125 last year provides property-specific support for testing a higher asking rent. Hyland’s full renovation, private pool and three-car garage strengthen its positioning, with no age-based discount applied.

$4,350 is the upper end of our supported test range, not a guaranteed rental result. The comparable evidence does not support starting above this amount. It is $350 above the closest private-pool lease and matches the highest completed lease in this sample, a larger six-bedroom home that took 105 market days. This supports treating $4,350 as a short market test rather than an established achievable rent. Interest should give us an early indication of whether renters see sufficient value at that level.

We can launch at $4,350, assess qualified inquiries, tours and applications, and recommend changes based on the evidence. Strong marketing creates exposure; it cannot require the market to accept a price. If response is weak, $4,195 and the prior $4,125 rent are useful reassessment points—not guaranteed outcomes or minimum acceptable rents. A move toward $3,995 may be warranted if competing inventory and actual feedback support it. We should not wait through multiple slow reductions simply to defend the original asking price.

04Marketing time, readiness and the pricing plan+

Read the history before interpreting the days

CDOM is cumulative MLS marketing time. It is not verified vacancy and does not necessarily equal the time until rent begins. Harts Cove went pending after 10 days and recorded its lease six days later. Sailing Pier went pending after 13 days but recorded the lease 42 days after launch. An accepted application or signed lease can still precede actual occupancy by weeks.

Glenraven and 4379 Renly were tenant-occupied, so their 81 and 105 market days cannot be counted as 81 and 105 vacant days. Access, availability and price may all have influenced the outcome; the records do not isolate each factor’s effect.

05Effective income: why vacancy matters+

The $225 increase has a limited vacancy budget

Compared with last year’s reported $4,125 rent, asking $4,350 adds $225 monthly, or $2,700 over twelve fully paid months. At $4,350, daily gross rent is approximately $143.01 using annual rent divided by 365. About 19 additional vacant days would consume the entire annual premium.

This illustration assumes $4,125 could otherwise start immediately; last year’s result does not prove that is achievable today. It measures additional vacancy caused by holding out, not all marketing time. If the property remains occupied and rent-producing, marketing days are not automatically lost-rent days.

Effective rental income is the priority. Compare receipts after vacancy and concessions over the same period. Deduct owner-paid pool/lawn service, management fees and other operating expenses separately to evaluate net income. The 21-day leasing target is an operating goal, not permission to wait 21 extra vacant days for a premium that only covers about 19.

Vacant days cannot be leased retroactively. We will let the market test $4,350, listen to its response and adjust promptly when the evidence calls for it.
